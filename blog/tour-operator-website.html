How to build a tour operator website: AI, a web developer, or a website builder?

By Blake Ng
Co-founder, Kong Prices checked 23 November 2026 ~20 minute read

There are three ways to build a tour operator website: with AI, with a web developer, or with a website builder. For most tour and activity operators, a website builder made for tours is the fastest and one of the cheapest, because live availability, booking and payment are already built in.

Here's the honest answer up front:

  • New, with one or two tours and no website: use a tour operator website builder. The site can be live this week. Taking payment waits on your payment account, so apply for that first.
  • Already have a website you like: keep it. Add a booking calendar to it. Don't rebuild.
  • Want something no builder can give you, and have the budget: hire a web developer, and connect a booking system to what they build.

I spent six years in product marketing at Rezdy, Checkfront and Regiondo before co-founding Kong, which is one of the website builders in this guide. I'm not neutral. I'll tell you where the other options are the better choice, because anything less would be useless to you.

Two things to know about Kong before you read on: we started in 2025, and we don't connect to Viator yet. If either rules us out for you, the guide still works. Prices are in US dollars, and there is one clearly marked section for operators in Malaysia, where we're based.

On answering questions: a WhatsApp button or live chat works when you're awake. A chatbot answers at 2am, and that's where it can go wrong. How to improve your tour website's conversion: an eight-point check

Conversion is the share of visitors who go on to book. Run these on your current site, or on any site you're about to pay for.

  • Can a stranger tell what the tour is, how long it takes and what it costs within ten seconds?
  • Does it say what is included and what is not?
  • Can they see open dates without messaging you?
  • Can they pay without leaving your site?
  • Does it load quickly on a phone, on mobile data?
  • Are there recent reviews from real guests?
  • Is the meeting point on a map?
  • Is there a way to ask a question?

If you answered no to the third or fourth, that's where your bookings are leaking.

What a tour operator website costs: three examples

For a small operator, a tour operator website costs about $700 to $2,100 in its first year with a builder made for tours, about $800 to $1,600 with Wix or AI, and $2,900 to $6,500 with a web developer, all in. One tour builder, FareHarbor, costs you $450, because your guests pay about $900 in booking fees on top.

Each table has two figures. You pay is everything that comes out of your pocket in year one: the website, the booking system, the booking fee, and card processing at about 3% of sales. (Stripe charges 2.9% plus 30 cents in the US and 3% plus RM 1 in Malaysia.) Your guests pay is any booking fee added on top of your price at checkout. Your own time isn't counted.

Already have a website? Start here

You do not have to rebuild. If your site looks fine and guests can find it, the only thing missing is a way to book and pay.

How to accept bookings on a tour website you already have

  1. Sign up for a booking system and add your tours, times, prices and how many people each one takes.
  2. Connect a payment account so money goes to you. That's an account with a payment provider such as Stripe.
  3. Copy the booking calendar code the system gives you. It's a few lines of text.
  4. Paste it into your tour pages in Wix or WordPress (this is called embedding), and test a real booking on your phone.

Your pages, your web address and your place on Google stay as they are, because you're adding a calendar to pages that already exist. If the calendar doesn't show up properly, the booking system's support team should help you place it. Ask before you sign up.

With Kong, the booking calendar can be embedded in a site you already have.

Start again instead if your site is slow or hard to use on a phone, you can't update it yourself, or you avoid sending people to it.

Method 1: Build it with AI

Building a tour operator website with AI costs about $2 to $25 a month and takes an afternoon. It works well for the pages. It doesn't give you live availability or payment, so you still need a booking system.

"Build it with AI" means two different things:

  • AI website builders. You answer a few questions and it makes a site. Wix includes AI tools in every plan. 10Web's starts at $10 a month billed yearly, and Hostinger's at $1.99 a month if you prepay four years.
  • Building from scratch with an AI tool. You describe the site to a tool like Lovable, Claude or ChatGPT and it writes it for you. Lovable Pro is $25 a month and lets you use your own domain.

What it's good at: a good-looking site fast, low cost, and changing words or layout by asking in plain language.

Where it falls down: an AI tool can make a booking form in seconds, but a form is not a booking system. It doesn't know the 3pm slot is full, and it doesn't stop two people booking the last seat. You can ask AI to build that too. A booking engine company called CaptainBook made a fair point about this in August 2026: building the first version is no longer the costly part. What comes after is. Payments, refunds and chargebacks (when a guest disputes a payment with their bank). Keeping the site up and secure. And someone to call when it breaks on a Saturday in peak season.

Even GoDaddy's AI builder page tells tour operators to embed a checkout from a booking system. So use AI for the pages and a booking system for the bookings.

It suits you if you enjoy this kind of thing and don't mind fixing it yourself. Avoid it if you want to set it up once and get back to running tours.

Method 2: Hire a web developer

Hiring a web developer for a tour operator website costs $1,500 to $8,000 for a small site and takes 2 to 8 weeks. You get a custom tour company website design, and you still need a booking system connected to it.

Tour operator website development doesn't end at launch. Budget for hosting, which is the service that keeps your site online ($40 to $150 a year), your domain ($12 to $25 a year), and paid changes.

What it's good at: a site built the way you want it, by someone else, with a design nobody else has.

Where it falls down: it costs the most, every change is a message and often a bill, and if the developer moves on you may be left with a site nobody can update.

Questions to ask before you hire:

  1. Which booking system will you connect, and what will it cost me each month?
  2. Can I change prices, photos and tour times myself, without calling you?
  3. Who owns the domain, the hosting account and the logins? (It should be you.)
  4. If we stop working together, what do you hand over?
  5. Can I see a tour website you built, and may I speak to that operator?
  6. What do you charge for changes after launch?

It suits you if you have a clear brand and the budget for it. Avoid it if you're just starting, or you change tours and prices often.

Your first week, and getting found

A website makes direct bookings possible. It doesn't bring them. Here's what to do first, and then how people find you.

Your first week

  1. Day 1: apply for a payment account. That's an account with a payment provider such as Stripe, which takes card payments and pays the money into your bank. You apply on the provider's website with your business and bank details. Approval can take days or weeks, so start here.
  2. Day 1: buy your domain, which is your web address, in your own name. Registrars such as Namecheap or GoDaddy sell them for about $12 to $25 a year.
  3. Day 2: gather what you'll need: ten good photos, a description of each tour, what's included and not, your cancellation policy, and your meeting point.
  4. Day 3: add one tour and get it right before adding the rest.
  5. Day 4: make a real booking on your own phone, with a real card, and refund it.
  6. Day 5: put your booking link everywhere guests already find you: WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook and your Google listing.

Plan on a few hours across the week with a builder made for tours, and longer with the other methods.

Getting found

  • Google Business Profile. It's free, and it's how most guests find a local tour. Claim it, pick the right category, add photos and put your booking link on it.
  • Reviews. Ask every guest on the day, and send the link while they're still happy. Recent reviews do more for bookings than a new design.
  • Guests you already have. Give repeat guests and referrals your direct link. If you sell on Viator or GetYourGuide, check their terms before you point those guests elsewhere.
  • AI answers. People now ask ChatGPT and Google's AI what to do in your town. Clear tour pages with prices, times and what's included are what those tools read.

Questions operators ask

What is the best website builder for a tour company?

For most small tour companies, a website builder made for tours is the best fit, because availability, booking and payment are built in. Kong has no monthly fee and charges 1.8% per online booking. FareHarbor has no monthly fee either, because your guests pay its booking fee of up to 6%. Bokun starts at $49 a month plus 1.5% and is the stronger choice if you sell mostly through Viator.

Should I use Wix or WordPress for my tour business?

Wix is easier to look after, but your site has to stay on Wix. WordPress gives you more control and can move between hosts, but someone has to keep it updated. Wix's own booking tool can cover a simple tour with a set number of seats. For anything more, add a tour booking system to either one.

Can I build my tour website with AI?

Yes, for the pages. AI tools can produce a good-looking site in an afternoon for $2 to $25 a month. They don't give you live availability, safe payment or protection against double bookings, so connect a booking system for those.

Do I need a web developer for a tour website?

Most operators don't. A website builder covers what a typical tour business needs for far less. A developer makes sense when you want a custom design and can spend $1,500 to $8,000 or more.

How much does a tour operator website cost?

For a small operator taking $15,000 a year online, year one costs about $700 to $2,100 all in with a builder made for tours, about $800 with Wix and its own booking tool, about $1,600 with AI plus a booking system, and about $2,900 to $6,500 with a freelance developer. Those figures include card processing at roughly 3%. FareHarbor costs the operator $450, with guests paying about $900 in booking fees on top.

How do I take bookings and payments on my website?

You need a booking system that tracks your availability, a payment account so the money reaches you, and a booking calendar on your tour pages. A builder made for tours gives you all three together. On an existing Wix or WordPress site, you paste in a booking calendar from a booking system.

Should a tour operator website have a chatbot?

Only if guests often message you outside working hours with the same questions. Choose one where you decide what it may answer, it contacts you for anything else, and you can take over the chat yourself.

What is the best website builder for tour operators in Malaysia?

Start with payment methods, because many Malaysian guests pay by FPX or e-wallet. Kong is based in Kuala Lumpur, has no monthly fee, and connects to Stripe, Xendit and HitPay. If you sell heavily through Viator, compare it with Bokun first.
