SEO for Tour Operators: The Australian Guide

Blake Ng
Co-founder, Kong Last updated 12 August 2026 16 min read

If you run tours and want more direct bookings, the highest-leverage work is not your website. It is your Google Business Profile: the listing that decides whether you appear in the map results when someone searches "kayak tour Byron Bay" standing on Jonson Street. The 2026 Whitespark Local Search Ranking Factors study found that Google Business Profile signals remain the strongest factor group for map rankings, with 8 of the top 10 individual factors coming directly from the profile (whitespark.ca, verified 7 Aug 2026). Reviews are second and rising.

Your website matters, but mostly as proof and conversion, one page per tour, done properly. And a new channel is arriving fast: 45% of consumers now use ChatGPT or other generative AI tools for local recommendations, up from 6% a year earlier (BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, a US survey of 1,002 consumers; verified 7 Aug 2026).

A note on the evidence before we start: the big consumer surveys are American, because that is where they run at scale. Treat the numbers as directional for Australia. This guide covers all of it, in the order that pays.

How guests actually find a tour operator

Strip away the theory and there are four doors a guest walks through.

Brand searches. Someone heard your name from a friend, a brochure, a hotel front desk, or saw your trailer at the beach. They search your business name. This is the door you must never lose: if your name search shows a competitor's ad, a wrong phone number or a stale profile, you paid for the awareness and lost the booking.

Google Maps and the Local Pack. The three map results above the normal listings. For any "thing to do + place" search, this is where the clicks concentrate; a FirstPageSage meta-analysis puts local pack click-through at 17.6%, 15.4% and 15.1% for positions one to three, 48.1% combined (firstpagesage.com, May 2025, verified 7 Aug 2026, secondary source). For a tour operator, ranking here is worth more than ranking in the blue links below it.

Ordinary organic search. "Best whale watching Hervey Bay", "what to do in Margaret River with kids". Your tour pages and content compete here, against OTAs with million-dollar SEO teams. You will not outrank Tripadvisor for "things to do in Sydney". You can absolutely win "sunrise kayak tour Noosa", which is the search a buyer makes.

AI assistants. Guests now ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude to plan trips. Among American travellers, 37% report using or planning to use AI to help plan holidays (Allianz Partners Global Travel Confidence Index, July 2026, verified 7 Aug 2026). Assistants draw on the same public sources Google reads: your profile, your reviews, your pages and the pages that mention you. You cannot buy placement. You can be the operator whose details are consistent, specific and everywhere the assistants look.

Notice what is not on the list: generic keyword blogging, domain authority campaigns, link exchanges. We will come back to what to ignore.

Google Business Profile: the highest-leverage asset you own

Google's own guidance says local ranking is a blend of relevance, distance and prominence, and that "businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results" (support.google.com/business/answer/7091, verified 7 Aug 2026). Distance you cannot change. Relevance and prominence are built by hand, field by field. Here is the pass to run on your profile this week.

The no-shopfront problem first. Most tour operators have no walk-in premises: you meet guests at a wharf, a trailhead or their hotel. Google handles this with service-area settings, and getting them wrong is the most common day-one blocker. If guests should not turn up at your home address, hide the address on the profile and set the areas you serve; if you have a real meeting point guests can visit, show it and put photos of it on the profile so guests recognise it. Run several departure points under one brand and you generally maintain one profile per genuine staffed location, not one per pickup spot; inventing locations you do not staff is the kind of thing that gets profiles suspended. Sort this before polishing anything else, because every other field hangs off it.

Categories. Your primary category is the strongest single lever on the profile. Make it the most specific match Google offers: "Kayaking service" or "Whale watching tour agency" or "Winery tour agency" rather than a generic "Tour operator" if a closer fit exists. Add every legitimately applicable secondary category. Operators routinely leave two or three relevant categories empty, which is rankings left on the table.

The experiences and products section. Google lets you list individual offerings with names, photos, prices and booking links. Most operators leave it empty. Fill it with every tour you run, priced in dollars, one entry each: this is free shelf space in the most valuable retail location on the internet, and it lets a guest see "Sunset Cruise, $89" before they have visited your website. Keep prices current; a profile showing last season's prices reads as neglect.

Keyword spread across fields. Your business description, your services, your Q&A answers and your photo captions are all indexed. Write them the way guests search: name the activity, the place and the audience. "Half-day 4WD beach tours from Airlie Beach, family friendly, maximum 12 guests" does work that "unforgettable adventures await" never will. Do not stuff your business name with keywords; Google suspends profiles for it, and the risk is not worth the edge.

Review velocity and replies. The 2026 BrightLocal survey makes the stakes plain: 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, 74% want reviews written in the last three months, 68% will only use a business rated four stars or above and 31% hold out for 4.5 (brightlocal.com, US survey, verified 7 Aug 2026).

Three practical consequences. First, a wall of glowing reviews from 2023 is a decaying asset; recency beats volume, so you need a steady drip, not a burst. Make the ask part of the tour's ending, the same sentence every time, while the guest is still glowing. Something as plain as "if today was worth five stars, it would mean a lot if you told Google, the link is in your confirmation text" works, because every guide can say it the same way. Keep the ask unconditional: no discounts or freebies for reviews, which protects you and keeps the reviews real.

Second, rating matters more than count once you clear a few dozen: protect it by fixing the operational things reviews complain about. Third, reply to every review, good and bad. Replies are indexed, they show future guests someone is home, and Whitespark's 2026 study reports review signals rising in weight for map rankings (verified 7 Aug 2026).

The Q&A field. Anyone can ask a question on your profile, and anyone can answer, including competitors and guessing strangers. Seed it yourself: post the ten questions guests ask on the phone and answer them properly. Wrong answers from the public sitting unchallenged on your own profile are a self-inflicted wound that takes minutes to fix.

Updates and offers. Google's post feature puts dated updates on your profile: seasonal tours, changed winter hours, a new departure point. Individually small, but a profile with fresh updates, fresh photos and fresh reviews reads as an open, active business to both Google and the guest comparing three operators on a Saturday night.

Photos, hours, phone, booking link. Recent photos from actual tours outperform stock. Wrong hours on a long weekend cost real bookings. And your booking link should take the guest straight to a bookable calendar, not a contact form; every extra step between the profile and a confirmed booking leaks guests.

What a profile rebuild can do

The compounding effect of doing every field properly is larger than operators expect. One example from our own work: a Cameron Highlands operator with a 5-star rating and 800+ reviews moved from position 8 to position 1 on Google Maps after a profile rebuild, with bookings up roughly 30% by the operator's own account {{SATU_SWAP: replace with named version only if approved}}. Nothing about the business changed; the profile simply started telling Google everything the business already was. Position 8 on Maps is invisible. Position 1 is a queue.

Full disclosure: that operator is in Malaysia, our home market, the result is self-reported, and an Australian case study will replace it as soon as one of our Australian operators has the history to show. The mechanism, though, is Google's algorithm, not geography: profile completeness and review signals rank the local pack the same way in Katoomba as in Malaysia (Whitespark 2026, verified 7 Aug 2026), and most Australian tour operator profiles are just as half-filled, so completing every field, listing every tour and keeping reviews flowing means competing against half-effort almost everywhere.

Your website: fewer pages, built properly

The website's job is to convert the demand the profile and reviews create, and to catch the searches too specific for a map result. Three structures do most of the work.

One page per tour. Not a "Tours" page with six tabs. Each tour gets its own page with its own URL, targeting the search a buyer makes: "Ningaloo whale shark swim", "Barossa wine tour from Adelaide". Put the answer above the fold: what it is, how long, how much, who it suits, next available date, book button. Then the detail: full itinerary, what to bring, cancellation policy, real photos, recent reviews. OTAs win generic searches; your tour page wins the specific one, and specific searches are the ones that book. Price openly on the page. Hiding prices behind an enquiry form loses the guest who is comparing three operators at 9pm, and from 1 October 2026 Australia's pricing rules make fee transparency at checkout a legal matter as well; our card surcharge ban guide covers what changes.

Location pages, if you genuinely serve multiple places. An operator running tours from both Cairns and Port Douglas should have a page for each, with real local detail: departure points, parking, what differs. Do not spin up thin pages for every suburb in a fifty-kilometre radius; Google treats near-duplicate location pages as the spam they are.

FAQ sections with schema. Every tour page should answer the questions guests actually ask: what happens if it rains, can my eight-year-old come, is there a toilet on the boat, what do I wear. Mark them up with FAQPage structured data so search engines can read them as questions and answers. The same material is what AI assistants quote when a traveller asks "is the sunrise tour or sunset tour better for kids in Exmouth". Write the answers in plain sentences that survive being lifted out of the page, because increasingly they are read aloud by a machine to a guest who never sees your website at all.

Two supporting habits. Keep your name, address and phone number identical everywhere they appear: profile, website, directories, tourism association listings. Inconsistency erodes the trust signals everything else builds. And get listed where Australian tourism already links: your regional tourism organisation, your state operator directories and the Australian Tourism Data Warehouse listing that many official destination sites draw from. Those are the citations and links that matter for a tour business; swapping links with a plumber in another city is not.

AI assistants: the channel forming now

The slope in the BrightLocal numbers from the introduction is the point: generative AI use for local recommendations multiplied more than sevenfold in a single year while Google use for evaluating local businesses dipped from 83% to 71% (BrightLocal 2026, verified 7 Aug 2026). Google is still the main game by a wide margin. But no channel in local discovery has ever grown that fast.

There is no AI equivalent of buying an ad yet, and no assistant publishes its ranking factors. What is observable: assistants lean on the open web. They read review platforms, your Google profile, your structured data and articles that mention you. So here is the honest version, said plainly: there is no separate AI playbook yet. The work is the same work, complete profiles, recent reviews, clear factual pages, quotable FAQ answers, consistent details everywhere, and the operators doing it are already positioned for this channel. The one genuinely new habit: ask an assistant your own money questions ("best food tour in Fremantle", "whale watching Hervey Bay which operator") every month or two and read what it says about you and your competitors. If it gets your prices, duration or meeting point wrong, the fix is usually a page or profile field on your side that is missing or stale.

What to ignore

Generic SEO advice is built for e-commerce stores and content sites, and much of it wastes an operator's year. Skip: publishing weekly blog posts nobody searches for, in the belief that volume itself ranks. Skip: paying for guest posts and link packages; one link from your regional tourism body outweighs a bag of them. Skip: obsessing over homepage keyword density, domain authority scores and site speed decimals while your Google profile sits half-empty; completeness of the profile is Google's own stated factor, your Core Web Vitals score is a tiebreaker at best. Skip: separate near-identical pages for every keyword variant ("kayak tour", "kayaking tour", "kayak trip"); Google collapsed that game years ago. And skip any agency that opens with "content strategy" before it has looked at your Business Profile categories. The order of operations for a tour operator is profile, reviews, tour pages, then everything else.

One caution the other way: none of this replaces the economics of your channels. SEO grows the direct channel precisely because direct bookings keep the commission an OTA would take, industry-reported at 20 to 30% for the major platforms (Arival; Samba HQ; verified 7 Aug 2026); the arithmetic, with every source, is laid out in our guide to what OTAs and resellers charge Australian operators. The operators who win run both deliberately: OTAs for reach and first-time guests, direct for margin and repeat business.

How to know it is working

Measure three things, monthly, and ignore the rest.

First, your Google Business Profile performance report: Google shows you how many people found the profile, what they searched, and how many called, asked for directions or clicked through. Watch the split between brand searches (people who knew your name) and discovery searches ("kayak tour" plus your town); growth in discovery searches is the local pack work paying off.

Second, your map position for your three money searches, checked from where your guests actually search, not just your own office, because positions vary with the searcher's location. The manual version is checking from your phone at the visitor strip or the caravan park end of town; the proper version is a local rank-tracking tool that checks positions from a grid of points across your area, which several SEO tools offer cheaply. Either beats assuming the position you see at your desk is the position a tourist sees.

Third, bookings from direct channels in your booking software, which is the number the whole exercise exists to move. Tag your profile's booking link with a UTM parameter so those bookings are attributable rather than folded into "direct traffic". If profile views rise but bookings do not, the leak is usually the landing page or the price; if neither rises after 90 days of real work, re-check your categories first, because the wrong primary category quietly caps everything else.

The 90-day order of operations

The sequence, in a form you can hand to whoever runs your marketing:

  • Days 1 to 14: fix the profile. Service-area settings sorted, correct categories, every tour in the products section with prices, rewritten description, current photos, hours checked, booking link pointing at a bookable calendar.
  • Days 15 to 45: build the review engine. Script the ask, make it part of every tour ending, reply to everything, watch recency.
  • Days 46 to 90: rebuild your top three tour pages. One page per tour, price visible, FAQ with schema.
  • Then hold the routine. Review replies weekly, profile prices each season, an AI assistant check monthly, map positions monthly.

None of it is clever. All of it compounds, and almost none of your competitors will do it consistently, because it is nobody's favourite job. That is exactly why it works.

FAQ

What is the most important SEO factor for tour operators?

Your Google Business Profile. The 2026 Whitespark Local Search Ranking Factors study found profile signals are the strongest factor group for map rankings, with 8 of the top 10 individual factors coming from the profile itself, and review signals second and rising (verified 7 Aug 2026).

How do I get my tour company to show up on Google Maps?

Complete every field of your Google Business Profile: the most specific primary category, all applicable secondary categories, every tour listed with prices, accurate hours and a steady flow of recent reviews with replies. Google's own guidance says complete, accurate profiles are more likely to appear in local results.

Do reviews really affect tour bookings?

Yes, on both rankings and conversion. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, 74% want reviews from the last three months and 68% will only use a business rated four stars or higher (verified 7 Aug 2026).

Should tour operators write a blog?

Only for questions guests actually search, such as seasonal guides and practical planning questions in your region. A weekly generic blog does little; a complete profile, strong tour pages and FAQ schema do more for both Google and AI assistants.

How do tour operators show up in ChatGPT recommendations?

There is no paid placement. Assistants draw on public sources: your Google profile, reviews, structured data and pages that mention you. Complete, consistent, factual information everywhere is the current playbook, and 45% of consumers now use generative AI for local recommendations (BrightLocal 2026, a US survey; verified 7 Aug 2026).

Is SEO worth it for a small tour operator?

The local work is, because it is cheap and compounding: profile completeness, reviews and proper tour pages cost time rather than money and directly grow the channel where you keep the margin OTAs would take. Generic SEO campaigns are usually not worth a small operator's budget.

How long does local SEO take to work for a tour business?

Profile changes can move map rankings within weeks; review recency builds over months; tour page rankings for specific searches typically take a few months to settle. Treat 90 days as the first honest checkpoint rather than expecting overnight movement.
