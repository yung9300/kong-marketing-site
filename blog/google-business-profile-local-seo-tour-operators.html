Google Business Profile & Local SEO for Tour Operators (2026)

Blake Ng
Co-founder, Kong June 2026 16 min read

Last updated: June 2026

For most tour operators in Malaysia, your Google Business Profile is your single most important booking channel — more international guests will find you on Google Maps than anywhere else. In fact, many of Kong's operators get around 60% of their direct bookings from their Google Business Profile. This guide shows you how to set it up properly and use local SEO to climb the Maps rankings, written for tour and activity operators specifically.

The short version: start with keyword research (find the 8–15 phrases guests actually search — Google autocomplete and a quick LLM prompt do most of the work), then claim and verify your profile, get your name/address/phone consistent, choose the most specific category, work your keywords into the Activities, Services and business description, add real geotagged photos often, pre-load your Q&A, build keyword-rich reviews steadily, and keep the profile active with weekly posts. More than half your Maps ranking comes from your profile and reviews combined — both free, both in your control.

These aren't theoretical steps. Using exactly this approach, we helped Let's Go Discovery, a jeep tour operator in the Cameron Highlands, climb from position 8 to position 1 in the local Google results for their key searches — and the bookings followed. The rest of this guide is the same playbook.

Why your Google Business Profile matters more than your website

When a traveller searches "things to do in Langkawi" or "ATV Selangor" on Google or Google Maps, it's your Google Business Profile — not your website — that decides whether you show up. For a tour operator, a well-optimised profile is the difference between being found by guests already looking to book and being invisible to them. It's no exaggeration to call it your most important booking channel: across Kong's operators, it's common to see around 60% of direct bookings trace back to the Google Business Profile.

This is why local SEO matters more for tour operators than traditional website SEO. Your guests aren't reading blog posts to find you; they're searching Maps for an experience in a place, right now, often on their phones while they're already in the country. Winning that moment is what local SEO is about.

What actually moves your Google Maps ranking

Before the steps, it helps to know what Google is actually weighing. According to the Whitespark 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors survey — a consensus of SEO practitioners, not an official Google statement, but the best proxy available — the rough weighting for Maps Pack ranking is: Google Business Profile signals ~32%, reviews ~20%, on-page/website ~15%, behavioural signals ~9%, links ~8%, citations ~6%, personalisation ~6%, and social ~4%.

The takeaway for a tour operator is simple: more than half of your ranking comes from your Google Business Profile and your reviews combined. That's where to spend your effort — and the good news is both are free and fully in your control. The steps below are ordered by impact.

Step 1: Do your keyword research first — it's the foundation everything else uses

Here's the mistake almost every operator makes: they fill in their profile with how they describe their business, not how guests search for it. You might call yourself a "premier outdoor experience provider"; your guest types "atv tour selangor." If your profile doesn't contain the words people actually search, Google has no reason to show you for them. So before you touch your profile, spend an hour finding the real phrases — then everything you write later (your description, services, activities, even how you nudge reviews) targets those phrases deliberately.

How to find your keywords — three quick methods:

  1. Google autocomplete and "related searches." Start typing your activity and location into Google ("kayaking langkaw…") and note what it suggests — those suggestions are real searches, ranked by popularity. Scroll to the bottom of the results page for the "related searches" and "people also ask" boxes too. Ten minutes here gives you the bulk of what you need, for free.
  2. Ask an LLM (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). This is the fastest method now and most operators don't think to use it. Prompt it plainly: "I run mangrove kayaking tours in Langkawi for families and couples. List the 15 phrases tourists would type into Google to find and book an experience like mine, ordered from most to least likely, including any local or Malay variations." You'll get a ranked keyword list in seconds. Refine it by asking for "booking-intent phrases" vs "research phrases," or for "what someone already in Langkawi would search vs someone planning from overseas."
  3. Read your competitors' reviews and the OTA listings. Look at how Klook, Viator and your top-ranked competitors title and describe the same experience — they've usually done the keyword work already. The exact phrases that recur (e.g. "sunset," "mangrove," "island hopping," "half-day") are the ones guests respond to.

Sort what you find into two buckets:

  • Money keywords — booking-intent, location-specific phrases: "atv tour selangor," "langkawi island hopping," "kl cooking class." These are what you want to rank for; they're guests ready to book.
  • Discovery keywords — broader research phrases: "things to do in langkawi," "cameron highlands activities." Useful, higher competition, but worth weaving in where natural.

Aim for a focused list of 8–15 phrases that genuinely describe what you sell. Write them down — you'll use this exact list in every step that follows.

Step 2: Put those keywords everywhere that matters — bio, services, activities and reviews

Finding the keywords is half the job; the other half is placing them in the specific fields Google reads. A keyword does nothing sitting in a notebook. Work your list into all four of these:

  • Your business description (bio): lead with what you do, where, and for whom, using your top money keywords naturally. "We run small-group mangrove kayaking tours in Langkawi and island-hopping trips for families and couples, with licensed local guides" puts three real search phrases in the first sentence.
  • Your Services section: name each service using a keyword phrase — "Private mangrove kayak tour," "Sunset island-hopping cruise," "Half-day cooking class" — rather than generic labels. (Full detail in the Services step below.)
  • Your Activities section: this is the big one for tour operators (it unlocks with the "Tour operator" category). Put a keyword in each activity's name and its description — Google reads both. (Full detail in the Activities step below.)
  • Your reviews: you can't write these, but you can steer them. When you ask a happy guest for a review, gently prompt the specifics: "if you enjoyed the sunset kayak tour, it really helps if you mention it." Reviews that naturally contain your keywords ("amazing mangrove kayaking trip") are a genuine ranking signal — Google reads review text — and they're more convincing to the next reader too.

The principle: pick your 8–15 phrases once, then make sure each one appears somewhere a guest (and Google) will see it. Don't keyword-stuff — everything still has to read naturally to a human — but nothing you write on your profile should be vague when it could carry a real search term.

Step 3: Claim and verify your profile

Start by claiming your business at google.com/business and completing verification. Until your profile is verified, you can't control how your business appears or respond to reviews — so this is the non-negotiable first step.

Before you create a new profile, search for your business name plus your town in an incognito window to check a profile doesn't already exist — Google often auto-generates one, and a past employee or even a guest may have created a duplicate. Search a few variations and any older business names too. If you find duplicates, claim and merge them; competing profiles split your reviews and confuse your ranking.

Step 4: Get your name, address and phone exactly consistent (NAP)

Your business name, address and phone number — "NAP" in local SEO — must be identical everywhere they appear: your profile, your website, your Facebook page, every directory and booking platform. Google cross-checks these, and inconsistencies (a street spelled out on one listing and abbreviated on another, or an old phone number lingering on a directory) quietly erode your ranking.

Use your real business name as it appears on your signage and registration. Don't stuff keywords into the name field ("ABC Tours - Best ATV Langkawi") — it's against Google's guidelines and can get your profile suspended.

This matters more than it sounds, because for many operators the profile is the whole shopfront: in our 2026 study of 252 Malaysian operators, 74% of Malaysian tour operators have no website of their own (Kong, 2026, n=252) — they're reachable only through a channel like this one.

A note for tour operators specifically: if guests don't come to a fixed address — if you meet them at a pier, a trailhead, or a hotel lobby — set your profile up as a service-area business and hide the street address, or guests will type your name into Maps and drive to your office instead of the meeting point. This is one of the most common and costly mistakes in tour-operator profiles.

Step 5: Choose the right primary category — it decides which searches you appear in

Your primary category is the single strongest signal Google uses to decide which searches you show up in, and Google increasingly serves niche categories to niche queries. "Tour operator" works as a base, but a more specific category that matches what you actually run will usually win you more of the right searches. Here's the honest method the best GBP consultants use: search your main experience plus your town in incognito, look at the top three in the Map Pack, and note which category Google assigns them. That's your target.

Google has thousands of categories, and many tour types have an exact match. Real categories that exist as of 2026 include:

Step 6: Add additional categories — you can have up to nine

Beyond your primary, Google lets you add up to nine additional categories, and you should use them — each one is another set of searches you can appear in. But Google gives the primary category disproportionate weight, so don't dilute yourself: add only categories that genuinely describe what you offer.

For a kayaking operator who also rents kayaks and runs the occasional sunset cruise, that might be primary "Canoe & kayak tour agency" plus additional "Canoe & kayak rental service," "Tour operator," and "Boat tour agency" — four real categories, not nine padded ones. Relevance beats quantity; a tight set of accurate categories outranks a long list of loosely-related ones.

Step 7: Set "Tour operator" and fill the Activities section — the feature most operators miss

This is the single most underused feature for tour operators. When your category is set to Tour operator, Google unlocks an Activities section on your profile where you list your individual bookable experiences. Most operators never set the category, so they never see it — which means simply using it puts you ahead of competitors who've left it empty.

Treat it like a menu of what you sell. Add each experience as its own activity — the ATV jungle trail, the sunrise jeep tour, the half-day cooking class — with a clear name, the duration, a "from RM[price]" starting price, and a short description built around the keywords from your Step 1 list ("mangrove kayaking tour," "Langkawi island hopping," "KL street food tour"). Put a keyword in both the activity name and its description — Google reads both. This is where a browsing guest decides you're worth booking, so make each one read like the start of a great trip.

Step 8: Fill the Services section and business description

Separate from Activities, your profile has a Services section and a business description, and most operators leave both thin. Both are real ranking and matching signals — fill them properly.

In Services, list each thing you offer as a named service using the terms guests search — "private boat charter," "sunset cruise," "group cooking class," "mangrove kayak tour." Add a short description to each. Services and Activities overlap, but filling both gives Google more matchable, keyword-rich content and leaves no field empty.

Your business description (up to 750 characters) should lead with what you do, where, and what makes you worth booking — and work in your main keywords naturally rather than wasting the space on vague lines that could describe any business. "We run small-group mangrove kayaking and island-hopping tours in Langkawi with licensed local guides" beats "We are passionate about creating unforgettable experiences."

Step 9: Add real, geotagged photos — and keep adding them

Photos are one of the strongest ranking and conversion levers for tour operators — some GBP auditors rate photo quality as a better predictor of Map Pack performance than review count. But there's a right way to do it:

  • Show the actual experience, not your logo or office. Your cover photo should capture the trip itself — guests on the boat, the view from the ATV trail, the food on the table. Google's "at work" photo category (guides and guests in action) builds the most trust.
  • Use original photos, never stock. Google can detect stock images and it undermines whether you look like a real, active business.
  • Geotag your photos to the location you operate in — it's a direct signal that helps Google verify where you are and serve you to nearby searchers.
  • Keep adding them. Don't upload ten once and stop. Post a few after every tour — you're creating great photo moments daily, and fresh photos signal an active business. Competitors with 100 recent photos beat profiles stuck on ten from 2019.

Step 10: Set up Q&A before guests ask

Your profile has a Q&A section, and most operators ignore it — which means random strangers sometimes answer questions about your business, often wrongly. Get ahead of it: post and answer your own most common questions ("Do you offer hotel pickup?", "Is it suitable for beginners?", "What should I bring?"). This pre-loads helpful, keyword-rich answers, builds trust, and saves you repeat WhatsApp messages. Monitor it weekly, answer new questions fast, and upvote the most useful ones so they sit at the top.

Step 11: Gather reviews steadily

Reviews are around 20% of your ranking and a huge trust signal, and recency and steady flow matter as much as the total count. The single best ongoing thing you can do for your ranking is build a steady stream of genuine reviews from happy guests.

Because this is such a big lever, we've written a full companion guide on exactly how to do it — including the QR-code trick and how to respond to negative reviews: How to get more Google reviews for your tour business.

Step 12: Keep the profile active with Google Posts

Google favours profiles that show signs of life, and a dead profile makes travellers wonder if you're still operating. Use Google Posts to keep it active: post a seasonal offer, a new tour, a recent photo, or an update every week or two. Keep your hours current (especially around holidays), answer every question, and respond to every review. An active profile outranks a neglected one, all else equal — and this is genuinely most of what "local SEO" means for a tour operator. You don't need a technical background; you need to set the profile up well and keep it alive.

Why this matters more in 2026

With Visit Malaysia 2026 driving record international visitor numbers, the operators who show up on Google Maps will capture a disproportionate share of that demand. A traveller deciding what to do tomorrow is searching Maps tonight — and your profile is how you get in front of them at exactly that moment. Getting your local SEO right before and during the campaign is one of the highest-return things you can do.
