How to Build a Website for Your Tour Business (2026)

Blake Ng
Co-founder, Kong Last updated: June 2026 12 min read

If you run tours or activities — ATV trails, white water rafting, cooking classes, jeep tours, fishing charters, firefly cruises — and you've searched "how to build a website," this guide is for you. But I want to save you the most common mistake first, because I've watched a lot of operators make it.

Why you can trust this guide (and the one shortcut worth knowing)

I've spent years helping Malaysian tour and activity operators get online and take bookings, and I co-run a website-and-booking platform called Kong, which I'll mention in this guide. I'm telling you that up front because the rest only works if you trust I'm being straight.

So here's the straight version: most "how to build a website" advice is written for businesses selling things. A tour operator isn't selling things — you're selling seats on the 9am departure. That difference changes everything about what your website needs to do, and it's the part the generic guides get wrong. The biggest mistake I see operators make is spending weeks (and real money) building a beautiful website that can't actually take a booking — and then losing the international guests who wanted to book and pay on the spot.

This guide walks through your real options, what each costs, how to actually build it step by step, and — most importantly — what makes a tour website convert a looker into a paid booking, which is the only thing that matters.

For context on how rare that last part is: in The Digital State of Malaysian Tour Operators 2026, 74% of Malaysian tour operators have no website of their own (Kong, 2026, n=252) — so getting online at all already puts you ahead of most of the market.

TL;DR — the fastest path for your situation

Pick by what you need

  • Just want to take bookings, fast and free, no tech skills? → A booking-native builder like Kong: a real site and bookings in one, free, live in an afternoon. The trade-off is a Kong web address rather than your own domain.
  • Want a fully branded site on your own .com with a blog? → A generic builder (Wix or Squarespace) — but you'll add a separate booking system, pay a monthly fee in US dollars, and put in real setup time.

The honest, detailed version — including the step-by-step build and the conversion essentials — is below.

What a tour operator's website actually needs to do

Before you build anything, get clear on the three jobs your web presence has to do. Most operators overspend on the first and underinvest in the second.

  1. Show people what you offer and who you are — your experiences, your story, photos that sell the trip. An About page and good galleries.
  2. Take bookings and payments — let a guest anywhere in the world reserve a spot and pay. This is the part generic website builders don't do on their own.
  3. Help people find you — mostly your Google Business Profile, which is separate from your website (more on that at the end).

Your options, honestly

There are three realistic ways for a tour operator to get a website, and the right one depends on what you actually need.

Option 1: A generic website builder (Wix, Squarespace, WordPress). These are great for a fully branded site on your own domain, with a blog and full design control. The catch for tour operators: they don't take bookings on their own — you have to bolt on a separate booking system — and they take time, some tech skill, and a monthly subscription. If you want a polished brand site and you're willing to manage the booking side separately, these are solid. Many operators do want one eventually. Option 2: A booking-native website builder (this is Kong). Build a real multi-page site — a Home page, an About page, pages for your experiences — with bookings built in natively rather than bolted on. It's free and you can be live fast. The honest trade-offs: you get a Kong web address (yourbusiness.bookwithkong.com), not your own custom .com, and it's built for your site and bookings, not blogging or SEO content. For a non-tech-savvy operator who wants to be online and taking bookings quickly, this is usually the fastest path.

Option 3: Hire someone to build it. A developer or agency gives you the most polished, fully custom result — and it's the most expensive, usually overkill when you're starting out.

Why "bookings built in" matters more than design

Here's the part most "how to build a website" guides miss for tour operators: a beautiful website that makes guests message you on WhatsApp to book will quietly lose you international guests. Travellers from the UK, US, Japan, Australia and Germany often don't use WhatsApp and won't download it to book a tour. If they can't book and pay on the spot, many simply move on.

That's the real reason a booking-native builder beats a generic one for this audience. It's not about design — generic builders can look great. It's that your website is only doing its job if a guest can complete a booking on it, and that's exactly what a booking-native builder is built for. And it's the step most operators miss: per Kong's operator research, among operators that do have a live website, 70% still cannot take a booking on it — the site takes enquiries only (Kong, 2026, n=66).

What actually makes a tour website convert (most guides skip this)

This is the section I'd tear out and hand to every operator if I could. Every "how to build a website" guide tells you how to build a site. None of them tell a tour operator what their site needs to do to turn a curious visitor into a paid booking — which is the only thing that matters. Here's what actually moves the needle, in rough order of impact:

  • Put a real booking option above the fold. The single biggest leak is making people hunt for how to book. A clear "Check availability" or "Book now" button visible the moment the page loads — not buried at the bottom — is worth more than any design flourish. If a guest has to message you to find out times and prices, you've already lost a chunk of them.
  • Show live availability and prices, don't hide them. "Contact us for rates" kills bookings, especially with international travellers comparing options at 11pm. Showing your real prices and open dates builds trust and lets people book in the moment. Travellers expect to see the price before they commit, the way they do on Klook or GetYourGuide.
  • Lead with photos and video of the actual experience. Not stock images, not your logo — the trail, the boat, the food, real guests mid-experience. For a tour, the photos are the product; they're what make someone picture themselves there. A short video of the experience converts even better.
  • Make trust obvious and immediate. Travellers booking an adventure activity in a foreign country are nervous. Put your Google review star rating and a few real review quotes near the booking button, state your safety credentials and experience plainly, and show real faces of your guides. Trust signals next to the "Book" button lift conversion more than almost anything else.
  • Write for the traveller's actual questions. Duration, what's included, what to bring, fitness or age suitability, pickup or meeting point, cancellation terms. Answering these on the page removes the friction that otherwise turns into a WhatsApp message you have to answer manually — or a booking that never happens.
  • Make it work on a phone. Most travellers browse and book on their phones, often while already in the country deciding what to do tomorrow. If your booking flow is awkward on mobile, you're losing the majority of your traffic. (This is a hidden advantage of a booking-native builder — the booking flow is built mobile-first, rather than a desktop plugin squeezed onto a phone.)
  • Capture the booking, not just the enquiry. A "request a quote" form is a booking you might get. A live booking-and-pay flow is a booking you have. Every step between "I want to do this" and "it's paid for" is a place people drop off — so the fewer steps, the more bookings.

The honest takeaway: a tour operator's website lives or dies on whether a nervous traveller from another country can see the price, trust you, and book on their phone in under two minutes. Pretty design helps, but it's a distant second to that. This is exactly why "bookings built in" matters more than which builder has the nicest templates.

How to actually build it: a step-by-step

Enough theory — here's the actual process of getting from nothing to a live site taking bookings. This works whichever route you choose; we'll note where Kong makes a step disappear.

  1. Plan your pages before you build anythingDon't open a builder and start dragging boxes. Sketch your site first — most tour operators need just four or five pages:
    • Home — what you do, where, your best photo, and a booking button immediately visible.
    • An experience page for each tour — one per product (your ATV trail, your sunset cruise), each with its own photos, details, and booking option. This is where bookings actually happen, so give each experience its own page rather than cramming them onto one.
    • About — your story, your guides, your safety record. This is your trust page.
    • Contact / FAQ — meeting point, how to reach you, the common questions.
    That's a complete tour-operator site. You don't need more to start.
  2. Get your assets together firstThe thing that actually slows operators down isn't the building — it's not having the content ready. Before you start, gather: 10–20 strong photos of your real experiences (not stock), a short paragraph of your story, the details for each tour (duration, price, what's included, what to bring, meeting point), and your logo if you have one. An afternoon collecting these makes the build itself quick.
  3. Build the pagesOn a generic builder, you'd pick a template, then customise it. On Kong, you assemble each page from modules — a hero image at the top, text blocks for your story and details, image galleries for your photos, and product/product-grid modules that are the actual bookable experiences. Lead each page with a strong image and put a booking option high up.
  4. Set up your bookable experiencesThis is the step that's either the hardest or the easiest, depending on your route. On a generic builder, this is where you stop, choose a separate booking system, sign up for it, and wire it in — the part that defeats most operators. On Kong, your experiences are the booking system: you add each tour as a product with its price, your capacity per session, available dates and times, and any deposit, and it's bookable the moment it's on the page. No integration, no second tool.
  5. Connect paymentsTo take money you need a payment processor. On Kong you connect your own Stripe account (available in Malaysia) or Xendit (which adds local methods like FPX, Touch 'n Go, ShopeePay and DuitNow QR), and the money is paid into your own connected account. Guests pay a small 1.8% fee at checkout on online bookings; you keep the rest, and walk-in, phone and WhatsApp bookings are free.
  6. Test a booking yourself, then go liveBefore you share it, run a test booking end-to-end — book one of your own experiences, check the confirmation email arrives, make sure the price and details are right. Then publish, and put the link everywhere: your Google Business Profile, your Instagram and TikTok bios, your WhatsApp status.

The honest difference: on a generic builder, Steps 4 and 5 are where most operators stall for weeks (or give up and go back to WhatsApp). On a booking-native builder they're part of building the page. That's the whole reason this category exists.

What to put on an experience page (the page that actually earns the booking)

Your individual experience pages are where the money is made, so they deserve more than a photo and a price. The ones that convert all include, roughly in this order:

  1. A strong hero image or video of the actual experience at its best moment.
  2. A one-line promise — what the guest gets, in their words ("Half-day mangrove kayaking through Langkawi's UNESCO geopark, small groups, licensed guides").
  3. The booking option, high up — price, duration, and a "Check availability" button before they have to scroll.
  4. What's included and what to bring — removes the questions that otherwise become messages.
  5. The practical details — meeting point, start times, fitness or age suitability, what happens if it rains.
  6. Trust — your Google rating, a couple of real review quotes, your safety note.
  7. A gallery lower down for the guests who want to see more before committing.

Write it the way you'd explain the trip to a friend, not in marketing-speak. Specific and warm beats polished and vague every time — "you'll paddle through mangrove tunnels and probably spot eagles and monkeys" sells better than "an unforgettable nature experience."

Niche notes by activity

Different experiences sell themselves differently online. A few honest pointers:

  • ATV and off-road operators: galleries of the trails, the gear and happy riders do the selling; pair them with a booking that sets your capacity per session so you're never oversold.
  • White water rafting operators: an About page that builds trust on safety and experience matters as much as the photos; take deposits and set group sizes at booking.
  • Cooking class operators: galleries of the food and the setting; small-group sizing and capturing dietary notes when guests book.
  • Jeep and 4x4 tour operators: showcase the routes and scenery; set clear departure times for each session.
  • Fishing charter operators: photos of the catches build credibility; deposits protect you against no-shows on a fuelled, crewed trip.

Don't forget how people actually find you

One last thing, because it's the most common mistake: your website is not how most guests will find you — your Google Business Profile is. When someone searches "ATV Selangor" or "things to do in Langkawi," it's your Google profile, not your website, that puts you on the map. Frequently asked questions

Do tour operators need a website?
Not necessarily a full one. What a tour operator actually needs is a way to be found (a Google Business Profile) and a way to take bookings (a booking page). You can have both without building a traditional website — though a simple site with an About page and galleries does help guests trust you.
Can I build a website that takes bookings without hiring a developer?
Yes. Booking-native builders like Kong let you build a multi-page site with bookings built in, free, with no developer and no code. Generic builders like Wix and Squarespace can also be used without a developer, but you'd add a separate booking system on top.
What's the best website builder for a tour operator?
It depends on what you need. For getting online and taking bookings fast and free, a booking-native builder like Kong fits best. For a fully custom-branded site on your own domain with a blog, a generic builder like Squarespace or Wix (plus a booking add-on) is better. Many operators start with the former and add the latter later.
Can I build an About page and photo galleries on Kong, or just a booking page?
You can build a multi-page site — a booking page plus as many additional pages as you need, like a Home page, an About page, and pages for your experiences — using modules including text, image galleries, a hero image, product grids, and individual products guests book from. It's more than a booking page; it's a simple website with bookings built in.
Do I get my own domain name with Kong?
You get a Kong web address (yourbusiness.bookwithkong.com), not a custom domain like yourbusiness.com. If having your own custom domain matters to you, a generic website builder is the better fit for that specific need — and you can still use Kong for bookings.
How much does a website cost for a small tour business?
It ranges from free to a few thousand ringgit. A booking-native builder like Kong is free. Generic builders like Wix or Squarespace run roughly RM75–160 a month once you include a plan that takes payments plus a booking add-on. A developer-built WordPress site is typically around RM2,000–3,000 to set up in Malaysia, plus ongoing maintenance.
Do I need a website or a Google Business Profile first?
A Google Business Profile, in most cases. It's free and it's how most guests will find you on Google and Google Maps. A booking page is the close second so they can actually book. A fuller website is useful but can come after those two.
What makes a tour operator's website actually get bookings?
The essentials: a clear "Book now" or "Check availability" button visible the moment the page loads; live availability and real prices shown openly (not "contact us for rates"); photos and video of the actual experience rather than stock images; trust signals like your Google review rating and safety credentials right next to the booking button; answers to the questions travellers ask (duration, what's included, what to bring, meeting point); and a booking flow that works smoothly on a phone. A nervous traveller from another country should be able to see the price, trust you, and book on their phone in under two minutes — that's what converts.
Why can't I just take bookings over WhatsApp?
You can, but you'll lose bookings. Many international travellers (from the UK, US, Japan, Australia, Germany) don't use WhatsApp and won't download it to book a tour, and a "message us to book" flow loses the people who wanted to book and pay in the moment — including at 2am while you're asleep. A real booking page captures those bookings automatically; WhatsApp stays useful as one option among several, not your only one.
What pages does a tour operator website need?
Most tour operators need just four or five: a Home page (what you do, where, your best photo, a booking button up top), a separate experience page for each tour you run (each with its own photos, details and booking option), an About page (your story, guides and safety record), and a Contact or FAQ page (meeting point, common questions). That's a complete site — you don't need more to start.
How do I take payments on my tour website?
You connect a payment processor. On Kong you connect your own Stripe account (available in Malaysia) or Xendit, which adds local methods like FPX, Touch 'n Go, ShopeePay and DuitNow QR — and the money is paid into your own connected account. Guests pay a 1.8% fee at checkout on online bookings and you keep the rest. On generic builders, you'd sign up for a separate booking or payment tool and integrate it yourself.
How long does it take to build a tour booking website?
If your content is ready, a booking-native builder like Kong gets you live in a day — most of the time goes into gathering photos and writing your experience details, not the building itself. A generic builder plus a separate booking system takes longer because of the setup and integration. The slowest part for everyone is having your photos, prices and tour details ready, so prepare those first.
